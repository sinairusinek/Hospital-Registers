§ 02

What stood in Haifa before the Government Hospital

Haifa's hospital landscape was missionary and communal before it was governmental.

  • St. Luke's Hospital (Anglican, Jerusalem & the East Mission). The J&EM was founded in 1887 after Bishop George Blyth split from the CMS; Alexander Schapira led the Haifa mission. Registered in 1891 as a mission to Jews, it in practice treated 50 Muslims, 18 Jews and 20 Christians that year; in 1894–95 it was presented to the Ottoman authorities as serving "the poor and sick of all religions," with 40 beds, men's and women's wards, a pharmacy and an operating room. Construction finished 1896. In 1898 it treated 3,543 patients (1,367 Muslims, 1,269 Christians, 907 Jews). Reopened after the First World War in 1922 with 30 beds, 600 inpatients and about 5,000 outpatients drawn from Haifa and thirty-odd surrounding villages. Mahmoud ch.3 pp.121–131
  • The Deutsche Spital (Diakonissen-affiliated) served the German community of Haifa exclusively, in contrast to St. Luke's. Mahmoud diss. p.154
  • The Deutsches Hospital der Borromäerinnen — the Borromean Sisters, Catholic, under the Deutscher Verein vom Heiligen Land of Cologne — tells its own history in the Templer weekly. The sisters took over a pilgrim house in 1888 and ran a poor-dispensary; in 1898 they rented a neighbouring house that had served the Templer community as schoolhouse and prayer hall, and later bought and enlarged it when the Hejaz railway brought sick European (Italian) labourers. Dr Peters directed it to 1913, then Dr Wilhelm Hoffmann, a grandson of the Templer colony's founder. Admissions rose from 155 in 1899 to 760 in 1925. It served the German communities of Haifa, Waldheim and Bethlehem of Galilee first, but was "fast immer überfüllt."
  • The Italian Hospital dates from 1907. PD-ch4 pp.135–136
  • Mahmoud positions the Saraya prison and its prisoner-treatment arrangements as the true institutional pre-history of state medicine in Haifa — the niche the Government Hospital later filled. Mahmoud ch.3 pp.133–142
A third witness on St. Luke's primacy

The Borromäerinnen's own account says the sisters began nursing because "das bis dahin einzige Krankenhaus — das englische Missionshospital" could not meet the demand: before 1888, the English mission hospital was the only hospital in Haifa. This is a German Catholic source, independent of both the Anglican record and Mahmoud, agreeing with both. WdT 15 Apr 1926

There was a government hospital in Haifa before ours — in requisitioned German premises

The same article follows the Borromäerinnen hospital through the war. Requisitioned for German, Austrian and Turkish troops, it became a local sickbay and then a military hospital in 1918; on the British entry (September 1918) it was "als erstes Haus in Beschlag genommen." For over three years it passed through English, French and American hands, "in der letzten Zeit Regierungshospital", and was released only in January 1922, reopening to patients three months later.

So the Mandate government ran a Haifa hospital out of German premises until January 1922, eight years before our register opens and a decade before it took the St. Luke's building (§05). This is the prehistory of the institution, not its origin — but it narrows the unevidenced stretch to 1922–1930 and shows the government had already been in the hospital business here, in premises belonging to someone else, before it rented the mission's.

A confessional discrepancy to resolve

Mahmoud's "Deutsche Spital" is Diakonissen-affiliated, i.e. Protestant; the Warte's hospital is the Borromäerinnen, Catholic. Either Haifa had two German hospitals or one of the attributions is wrong. The al-Difaʿ tally of 7 July 1938 (§10) counts a single "German hospital" taking eight of the wounded — which does not settle it. Establish which before citing either.

A date to correct

PD-ch4 (pp.135–136) dates the Anglican St. Luke's "1899–1928." Both ends are wrong: Mahmoud has construction complete in 1896, and the closure was 1929 (§03). An unsourced Haifa cycling blog repeats "1889–1928"; it should not be relied on (§09).

§ 03

St. Luke's closes, March 1929

Bishop Rennie MacInnes announced the closure in the mission's own newsletter, quoted at length in the press:

"St. Luke's Hospital, Haifa … at the end of March. This step — the Bishop writes — has become necessary; because in these days we simply cannot afford it. When we re-opened the Hospital in 1920 we had practically a clear field, but now there are several fine and well-equipped hospitals and there is far more provision in Haifa both for in-patients and for dispensary patients than is actually needed."

PB, 11 March 1929 · · headline "ST. LUKE'S HOSPITAL TO BE CLOSED"

The Bishop adds a second, missiological reason: the expenditure was not justified "for the comparatively small number of patients who come to us … in a place where there is ample medical provision for all," relative to the missionary work it enabled.

Mahmoud independently dates the closure 31 March 1929 from the MECA archive ch.3 p.131 — an exact agreement between an archival source and a contemporary newspaper, reached separately.

His accompanying thesis matters for framing: St. Luke's had been "an alternative to a government or municipal hospital … and Haifa did not know such a hospital until the founding of the Government Hospital in the 1930s."

§ 04

Before 1933: small, rented, on Mountain Road

The Government Hospital already existed while St. Luke's was still open — the J&EM annual report for 1928–29 calls it "recently established" and "on the verge of adding private wards for the British," when Haifa had some 35,000 people, five hospitals and about seventy doctors, "exceeding requirements" Mahmoud ch.3 p.112 n.394. So the Government Hospital did not begin in St. Luke's.

Its scale and clientele in 1930 were modest and narrow:

"In Haifa, the hospital of the Government, containing 30 beds for general cases, can seldom take in patients other than officers and employees of the Government, including railway employees, and policemen, prisoners, and accident cases. The Hadassah (Jewish) Hospital of 50 beds is insufficient for the Jewish [population]."

MR-1930 · Health section §15

Its location was Mountain Road — Arabic طريق الجبل (Tariq al-Jabal), Hebrew רחוב ההר, renamed שדרות האו״ם after 1948 and שדרות הציונות (Zionism Avenue) in 1975. Three independent press attestations:

  • "Some 150 patients transferred from the former hospital wards on Mountain Road are already housed in the new premises."
  • "At the Old Government Hospital (Mountain Road) where an Emergency Aid Post is to be established…"
  • Air-raid casualty posts listed as "Government Hospital, Old Government Hospital" — two distinct places.

A separate Government Isolation Hospital stood on Mountain Street between its junctions with Khury and Catoni Streets , quoting a Gazette traffic regulation and corroborated by a dentist's advertisement . In 1932 that infectious hospital "was enlarged, temporarily, by taking the adjacent premises in use" .

Two archive files sharpen the picture of what stood on Mountain Road. A water-supply file running 1920–1936 is titled "Old Haifa Hospital & Main Disinfecting Station" — the title is the finding, naming the two as a single establishment for the whole pre-Bat Galim period . And a site-selection file for a new isolation hospital, 1925–1931, notes in passing an Infectious Diseases Annex at the Municipal Hospital in 1927 — a third institution in the frame, distinct from both the Government Hospital and the lazaret, and a reminder that "the hospital" in a Haifa source of the 1920s needs identifying before it is used.

§ 05

1932–33: the Government takes the St. Luke's building

This is the pivot, and the government states it plainly.

Public works "begun in 1932 and not yet completed: … alterations to Quarantine Lazarette, Haifa; alterations and repairs to St. Luke's Hospital, Haifa; additions and improvements to Central Prison, Jerusalem."

MR-1932 · Public Works §19

"The expansion of Haifa Hospital by 50 beds in the St. Luke's building was accomplished early in 1933 and has been of great utility to all communities in the district. Owing to the large increase of the European population of Haifa and of its labouring classes, the staff of the Hospital has had to work under pressure and every available bed has been required. The Government hospital at Acre has been closed."

MR-1933 · Hospitals and Dispensaries §7

So the Public Works Department renovated the vacated Anglican mission hospital through 1932, and it opened as fifty Government beds in early 1933. The premises were rented, not owned:

"…the severe pressure on the accommodation at present rented necessitated the construction of a pavilion ward, providing an additional 20 beds."

MR-1935 · Hospitals and Dispensaries §19

Which fits the mission retaining ownership and deliberating over disposal in exactly those years: the Bishop in Jerusalem (Graham Brown) put "the disposal of St. Luke's property at Haifa" under consideration, proceeds to fund a boys' school , and MECA holds 119/1 "Correspondence re: St Luke's property, Haifa, 1933–1937" .

The school was duly built elsewhere — foundation stone laid by the Bishop of Portsmouth on the Carmel slope near Khayat Beach ; MR-1937 §99 dates it "9th of April" and notes it opened for the October term. Hence every later "St. Luke's, Haifa" in the press means the school, not a hospital — including Arabic مدرسة مار لوقا في حيفا Filastin 11 May 1943.

The clinching sentence

"The new hospital at Haifa comprising 220 beds was opened in October, 1938, and patients from the old leased buildings were transferred to it." — the government confirming in one line that the pre-1938 premises were leased, that there were buildings plural, and that the move happened in October, not December.

Bed-count discrepancy

MR-1938 §15 says 220 beds. Secondary accounts of the December ceremony give 225. Prefer 220 — it is the primary source, and it is attached to the actual opening rather than the ceremony.

The construction file supplies the missing middle term. The 1935 bed allocation by section comes to 277 designed — 189 general and 88 fever — so the building's own drawings sit between the four hundred the 1935 report contemplated and the 220 that actually opened . The figures are not in conflict; they are three different things: an aspiration, a design, and an occupancy.

§ 07

The Bat Galim building, 1935–1938

  • A site was selected and schedules of accommodation completed for the architects in 1935; "a new hospital to accommodate four hundred patients is contemplated." Those schedules survive. The construction file holds the 1935 bed allocation by section (189 general + 88 fever = 277) and the 1936 Schedule of Accommodation, eight pages lettered A–V naming every ward block, section, theatre, store and staff quarter — the closest thing to a room list the building has left . Note what the 1935 design excluded: plague, cholera, yellow fever, smallpox and typhus. That omission is why a plague unit had to be built in 1942 (§08).
  • The site itself was expropriated from named owners — Edgar Clark and the heirs of Fuda Said — with cadastral plans at 1:2500 . Two maps bracket the change: H. Kendall's 1937 town-planning sheet showing the chosen site and the roads proposed to serve it , and a 1941 plan of the hospital and its environs at 1:1250 — the same shore before and after.
  • No ward-labelled plan has been found. The pavilions are lettered A–F throughout the correspondence, but no page says what any pavilion was for. What survives is the index to the drawings: PWD Drawing E/524 of 18 March 1941, a "Schedule of Received Record Drawings" listing some 65 as-built sheets and describing the fabric — six storeys, pavilions A–F, a service block with garage below and attendants' quarters above, numbered units 1–24 spread across the floors. Its last entry, E/R 286 "KEY PLAN," is the sheet that would name them, and it is not in the file. The schedule gives exact drawing numbers to request.
  • The six-storey main block was begun about July 1937 and topped out within four months: "the new Government Hospital facing the sea between Bat Galim and Haifa Town … erected on a sixty dunam site will include thirteen separate units complete with a power plant, engine rooms, garages, a laundry, morgue, refrigerating and disinfecting rooms, a butcher's shop…"
  • Electrical installation was contracted to Mr J. Weinshal ; the main contractor was Solel Boneh ; the Government allotted the work to Jewish labour under its labour-share policy . The construction file puts a figure and a dispute behind that policy. The Tenders Board worked a preliminary estimate on Solel Boneh's price at approximately £P.110,200 — against a 1935 sketch costing of some £P.77,000, the measure of how the project grew. And the contract carried a clause binding the company to employ Arab labourers who "should receive half of the sum of the total wages paid to the whole labour." On 18 September 1937, two months into the work and in the middle of the Arab Revolt, a petition drawn by the Haifa advocate I. A. Saadeh reached the Officer Administering the Government alleging the company had employed too few Arab labourers "nor paid even one tenth of the wages paid to the Jewish labourers," and asking for a neutral superintendent and returns of wages paid to each. No reply or decision appears anywhere in the file, and only those two pages of 1,569 touch the clause at all — so whether it was enforced is not answerable from this source .
  • Design is associated with Erich Mendelsohn in partnership with Dr Kempinski . Cf. Cesare Stevan (ed.), The Government Hospital in Haifa: Erich Mendelsohn and the Building Site (Milan, 1999). The Hebrew report of the ceremony has "the plan of the hospital was drawn by the engineer Mr A. Mendelsohn" — the initial is wrong, the attribution is not. Check against the standing Zawara/Mendelsohn correction in the Cathedra track before repeating. The archive supplies an epilogue: having wound up his Palestine practice and handed over the drawings, Mendelsohn offered to sell the Government the copyright, and on being told it did not wish to buy, warned that "as long as the copyright of the drawings remains the property of the architect, the client is not entitled to complete any building with it or to build any extension … nor entitled to copy or re-erect the whole building or parts of it or to publish any plans or photographs." The Government asked the Attorney General whether that would stand in a court of law; the file carries no answer (1 April 1941) .
  • Visibly complete by January 1938: "The Government Hospital at Bat Galim stands sheer, complete in architectural severity. Seen from the height of the old monastery, it throws up an arresting note, suggesting a newborn classic of the plain."
  • The High Commissioner inspected it on 5 January 1938, received by Colonel Heron, Mr Mendelsohn and Dr MacQueen.
  • It was built during the Revolt: a bomb was thrown at eight Jewish workers returning from the site , an armed band questioned the site watchman , and the contractor Mr Hetzroni "was just completing his contract for the new Government Hospital" when killed .
  • Access: Hospital Road was cut from western Kingsway for £P.800, approved 11 May 1938 ; completed at £P.700 over some 300 metres from the Bat Galim Road, forty men for thirty-five days .
  • At the ceremony, Mayor Hassan Bey Shukri said "the old Government Hospital was not compatible with the progress, development and expansion of Haifa" . The Vaad Leumi protested that no speech was delivered in Hebrew .

The Hebrew press carries the building from announcement to ceremony

The English record above gives the building's outline; the Hebrew dailies give it a continuous chronology, and they date the two things the English record leaves vague — when the announcement was made, and when construction actually finished.

  • Announced September 1935, at 260 beds. Doar HaYom, citing the Cairo al-Muqattam: the Government intends a large government hospital for Haifa, "the surgical department will hold 180 beds and the department for other diseases 80," work to begin shortly and take two years. Read against Haaretz of July 1933, which reports that "because the enlargement of the Government Hospital in Haifa was deferred, the position there became difficult in the intestinal-disease season" , the deferral and the answer to it are both on the record.
  • Contracted June 1937 at ~£90,000, with a 50/50 employment clause. The Department of Health signed with Solel Boneh; "among other conditions the Government stipulated that Solel Boneh employ 50 per cent Jews and 50 per cent Arabs." Ground was broken at Bat Galim within days, some 80 Jewish and Arab workers clearing and excavating.
  • What the clause was worth in practice. The Arab Workers' Society protested the award to a Jewish contractor ; in September the Arab workers on the site struck, telling the Deputy District Commissioner that their daily wage was 27–32 grush "while the wage of the Jewish carpenters comes to 60–75 grush a day." He promised to equalise them.
  • Working, equipped and counted five weeks before the ceremony. HaBoker of 18 November 1938 describes a hospital in the present tense: a 60-dunam site west of the port bought for some £41,000, equipment "transferred from the old hospital" plus £10,000 of new installation, pavilions for infectious diseases joined to the main block by a roofed passage, and "in all the Government Hospital has for the present 230 beds, among them 90 for infectious diseases." The trade ran its congratulations the same week — flooring, joinery, glazing, and Solel Boneh itself.
  • And the architect's own office dates completion to September. At the ceremony Dr Kampinsky, Mendelsohn's assistant, speaking in English, "gave details of the building work and said that its construction was completed in September of this year." Haaretz also puts the institution's annual budget at about £20,000.
  • The ceremony, 22 December 1938. The building cost £120,000; £100,000 was allocated at first and raised to £112,000 as materials rose, then enlarged again when "24 beds were added for patients from among the military." The High Commissioner's speech says 225 beds, "befitting the needs of this city better than the old hospital building," and closes: "I declare the hospital officially open, and I wish that it may remain empty." Mendelsohn was not there: the Director of Government Medical Services read out the architect's letter of regret, having taken leave.
  • The opening was a language incident as much as a medical one. Three papers carried the Va'ad Leumi's protest to the Chief Secretary that the speeches "were not translated into Hebrew," which "gravely offended the feelings of the Jewish public."
September finished, October occupied, December opened

The three dates now come from three independent directions — the architect's office (Kampinsky, reported 23 December), the registers (the NB20→NB21 seam of 31 October), and the Government's own ceremony (22 December). The November description of a working, equipped, 230-bed hospital sits between them and rules out reading the ceremony as the move.

The move is dated three independent ways, and they agree

This is the most securely dated event in the project.

  • The government. The hospital "was opened in October, 1938, and patients from the old leased buildings were transferred to it."
  • The press. "The Hospital which was to have been formally opened in December was rushed to completion and made ready for occupancy in October. Some 150 patients transferred from the former hospital wards on Mountain Road are already housed in the new premises. A resident physician, five doctors and 42 nursing sisters under the supervision of the Senior Medical Officer, Dr J. H. Thompson, comprise the present hospital staff."
  • The register's own binding. Notebook 20's last page ends with three admissions dated 31.10.38; Notebook 21's first page carries eleven more, all 31.10.38, continuing the annual serial at register no. 2686. One volume filled and the next began the same day — verified on both page images. Notebook 21 page 1 carries no heading of any kind: no inscription, no blank leaf, nothing marking a new building. The archive's physical bookkeeping records the move without ever mentioning it.

Who ran it — names the register does not carry

The admission registers name no member of staff, and until 1938 the printed record barely does either. From the move onwards the Palestine Post supplies a thin but real succession:

  • Dr J. H. Thompson, Senior Medical Officer at the move, over a resident physician, five doctors and 42 nursing sisters; the tour of the new building was led by Dr J. MacQueen.
  • Dr Naif Amin Hamzeh, Medical Officer, Government Hospital Haifa — in the Birthday Honours as Hon. M.B.E.
  • Dr M. K. M. Mishalany, Acting Senior Medical Officer, who with the Matron showed Field Marshal Lord Gort round the hospital during a three-day tour of the north; the High Commissioner chatted with a British constable and inspected the sections. The same notice places the District offices of the Department of Health inside the hospital building, and has Gort going on to the British Red Cross in the German Colony.

Two of the three names are Arab, in a British-run government hospital, at the senior clinical level — worth noting against the Kozma & Furas account of Palestinian doctors in government service.

The Hebrew press extends that succession backwards to 1931 and forwards to the last day, and it is dense enough to tabulate. Two men run through the whole of it: Dr A. Malchi, transferred in from Safed in October 1931 , the district's forensic physician through the 1930s , publicly thanked by a patient's family in a newspaper advertisement , out in 1940 to direct the infectious-diseases hospital at Bnei Brak and back by 1945 as director of the Haifa hospital itself ; and Dr Naif Hamzeh, giving evidence as a hospital physician at the trial over the 1 May 1936 disturbances , attempting with the chief physician Dr J. H. Thomson to separate conjoined twins born at Shefa-Amr in December 1940 , and the hospital's last director in April 1948 .

Consequence for the data

The corpus spans two different hospital plants, and the seam is 31 October 1938 — not the December ceremony. Any spatial, capacity or ward analysis must break there.

Unresolved

MR-1938 §88 dates the ceremony 21 December; the Palestine Post reports it as 22 December .

§ 08

The isolation hospital, and the lazaret question

Running beside the Government Hospital was a quarantine establishment the Arabic press calls الكرنتينا, المحجر الصحي, or مستشفى الحكومة للأمراض المعدية. Whether it was a separate institution or a department of the hospital is open, and it matters: it decides whether the admission register can serve as a denominator for the quarantinable diseases.

Nearly every attestation puts it beside the hospital — with its own director (Isa Matta), its own doctor and its own telephone. Filastin of 11 September 1941 names the hospital, the health offices and the Kerentina as three distinct things; the Palestine Post of 1933–34 has an Infectious Diseases Hospital at Haifa and a Government Isolation Hospital on Mountain Street. One source puts it inside: al-Difa' of 22 August 1946 has ill detainees moved . Evidence assembled in data/newspapers/lazaret_concordance.tsv (366 windows, Arabic and English).

Working hypothesis — narrowed, still not closed

The lazaret was a separate institution, and the 1946 phrase is loose usage: someone calling the hospital's own Isolation department "the quarantine." The two can coexist. For 1942–48 the Hebrew evidence below narrows it a long way, and the register can now be treated as the denominator for plague and smallpox in those years. For the 1930s the question stands open, and cholera (2 admissions in nineteen years) with it.

The archives have since been read, and they settle the shape of it. In 1928 the quarantine lazaret and an infectious-diseases block were a single planning problem: a Secretariat minute of 9 August names "two admitted needs" — enlarging the Haifa lazaret and building an Infectious Diseases Block — and holds that "it is clearly advantageous that the Infectious Diseases Block should adjoin the Lazaret." Both were sited, costed at £P.7,246 together, and deferred for want of provision in the Estimates . The land beside the harbour was nonetheless expropriated, at £P.277.5 per dunum . So the lazaret was real, adjacent by design, and institutionally distinct — the two were meant to sit side by side, which is exactly the arrangement that makes loose usage easy.

The 1930s question that remains is not whether a lazaret existed but where suspected cases were sent. The referral documents have not been found; until they are, cholera, plague and smallpox before 1942 stay uninterpreted.

But the hospital had quarantine capacity of its own, 1943–1945

Two Palestine Post items place infectious-disease provision inside the Government Hospital in exactly the years the register cannot see:

  • The Public Works Department's report of works for 1943–44 lists, among the things "useful in filling a few of the large and many gaps in the Government's health service," "a plague unit at the Haifa Government Hospital." The construction file dates and places it exactly. The 1935 design had deliberately excluded plague, cholera, yellow fever, smallpox and typhus; that gap was closed in the winter of 1941–42. The unit was discussed in December 1941, sketch-planned by 22 January 1942, costed at about £P.2,700, and built as a small masonry building hard against an existing verandah of the hospital — moved 90 cm clear of it to ease the foundations — as drawing B/1641, called in the papers both "Plague Unit" and "Plague Service Station" . It was therefore physically part of the hospital site, not a separate lazaret — which is what allows "Isolation Haifa" and "Govt. Hosp." to be read as one place in the 1942–44 returns.
  • "The first case of human plague in this area during the present season was admitted to the Government Hospital Isolation Section last week-end" — the patient named as Kurt Seidler, an internee of the German camp at Waldheim.

A second suspected bubonic case, "the second here within a fortnight," had been admitted to the Government Hospital for observation in July 1944, the previous patient having been cured of the disease and then died of heart failure.

This cuts against the simplest version of the lazaret hypothesis

If suspected plague were routinely diverted to the Kerentina, the Post would not be reporting plague cases admitted to a named Isolation Section of the Government Hospital, nor the PWD building a plague unit there. The two institutions evidently coexisted and both took such cases. That does not resolve the referral rule — it may have changed over time, or by severity, or by community — but it does mean "admitted at the lazaret instead" cannot be assumed as the explanation for a press report with no matching register row. The register may simply not survive for that month.

The Hebrew press says who built it, when, and where it stood

The Public Works Department's own review of its wartime building is explicit, and it is the single most useful sentence on this question in any language:

"The Department of Public Works took part in the operations against the plague epidemic. In 1942 the Department set up a special department for plague sufferers beside the Government Hospital in Haifa, and in the same year an isolation pavilion of 14 beds was built for the government hospital at Beit Safafa near Jerusalem." Further down, among the war years' works: "to the Government Hospital in Haifa a pavilion of 20 beds was added."

Haaretz, 10 July 1945 ·

Around that sit three further placements, all putting the work at the hospital rather than elsewhere in the town: a smallpox death in June 1944 "in the quarantine house beside the Government Hospital" ; the October 1947 cholera scare resolved "in the laboratory of the Government Hospital," where an old Arab from Wadi Salib was found not infected ; and the plague reporting below, which sends patients variously to "the isolation unit," "the department for infectious diseases of the Government Hospital" and "the department for infectious diseases beside the Government Hospital" — three phrases for one place.

And then the Yishuv's own statement of January 1948, made while arguing a quite different case:

"Incidentally: this hospital contains the only department for infectious diseases in the north of the country, and that is also the only department the Jewish population used."

HaMashkif, 26 January 1948 ·
For 1942–48, the register is the denominator

A plague department built by the Government at the hospital in 1942, a 20-bed pavilion added during the war, an isolation unit taking the 1944–45 cases, a laboratory settling the cholera scare, and the only infectious-diseases department in the north. Taken together these remove the hypothesis that Haifa's plague and smallpox cases went somewhere the register cannot see. The Arabic الكرنتينا is not thereby dissolved — a port quarantine station is a different institution with a different job — but it is no longer available as the explanation for a press report with no matching register row.

And a second consequence, for §14

If Jewish use of the hospital was "largely confined to one department," as the Yishuv's own committee says, then the register's confessional composition is not simply a measure of who lived nearby. It is partly a measure of which departments each community was willing to use — and the Rabbi of Haifa gives the reason in his own words: "the kitchen of the Government Hospital is absolutely treif." A religious barrier to use, stated plainly, and it belongs beside every figure in §14.

And it sharpens the dating problem below

The register's own Isolation ward disappears after February 1940, yet the hospital demonstrably still had an Isolation Section in August 1945. So whatever happened, it was not that isolation work left the hospital. The likelier reading is now the second one: the clerks changed what they wrote in the ward column.

The register's own Isolation ward vanishes — inside the gap

The register has an Isolation ward of its own: 2,278 admissions recorded as "Isolation", with a further 356 as "Infectious Diseases", together carrying a large share of the typhoid and diphtheria. It does not fade — the value disappears, and the ward vocabulary shifts with it (General falls away, British Section rises).

The Bat Galim move is not the cause: Isolation continues straight through the October 1938 seam, and the last isolation admissions fall in February 1940 — sixteen months after the hospital left Mountain Street. The change is undatable, because it falls inside the register's own four-year gap: normal on the last page before, extinct on the first page after. Two readings, neither preferred — the work moved out during the war years (which would fit a separate lazaret), or the clerks changed what they wrote.

§ 09

January 1948: the Jewish patients are removed

The German-language Mitteilungsblatt, organ of the Central European immigrants' association and searchable in full text through Compact Memory, reports from Haifa under the dateline 11 January 1948:

"Gleichzeitig mussten aus Sicherheitsgründen die jüdischen Kranken aus dem Regierungskrankenhaus herausgenommen werden!"

"At the same time the Jewish patients had to be removed from the Government Hospital for security reasons."

MB, 23 January 1948, p.4 ·

The same notice records Haifa short of 400 beds, a 70–80 bed emergency hospital opening in the Kupath Cholim rest home in Achuza, and sick and wounded lying in tents outside the Rothschild.

The register confirms it, to the month

Jewish admissions run at 20, 59, 12, 26 and 35 a month through 1947 — five to seven per cent of admissions. Then: 1 in January 1948, 2 in February, 0 in March, 0 in April. The three are dated 13 January, 2 February and 23 February 1948, after which there are none at all. An administrative act announced in a German-language weekly is visible in an Arabic-and-English ledger, and the two agree.

This is the strongest press–register corroboration in any language in the project, and it converts a statistical pattern into a documented decision. It also overturns a caution this document previously carried — that the near-absence of Jewish patients in 1948 was an artefact of thin coverage. It was not. It was policy (§14).

The Hebrew dailies date it, count it, and print an inquiry

The Mitteilungsblatt reports the fact from a fortnight's distance. The Hebrew press has the date, the numbers on both sides, and the findings of a commission the Haifa Situation Committee appointed — the closest thing this corpus offers to an inspection of the wards.

  • The date is 31 December 1947. "45 Jewish doctors and nurses left the Government Hospital yesterday afternoon for want of security, at the demand of the Yishuv's security committee. The employees did not respond to the request of the chief physician (an Englishman) to remain, and argued that nothing had been done to secure their lives and safety there."
  • A week later the count is larger and the ward is named. The strike of 76 Jewish employees, "among them 6 doctors and 47 nurses"; "there remain only six doctors, 16 Arab and Armenian nurses, and six Englishwomen," after which the management applied to the Red Cross and 27 Arab nurses arrived.
  • The trigger was the nursing school. Families of the Jewish patients demanded that they be moved, and the parents of the pupils of the hospital's nursing school feared for their daughters' lives. "As a result, on 31.12.47 all the Jewish patients were transferred out of the Government Hospital, and the medical personnel left their work."

The commission's findings, in its own order:

Many employees, "especially of the lower grades, appeared at the hospital armed with firearms; most also carry knives." Threats against the nurses and trainees by low-grade employees and by "the Arab policemen stationed there to guard the prisoners." "The wounded brought to the hospital are accompanied by their relatives and friends, or by armed gang members, and often a wounded man has armed bodyguards standing beside him day and night."

"In one case two shots were fired inside one of the hospital's lavatories." "In one case an Arab prisoner was brought to the hospital and was spirited out of it." "The hospital is enclosed by a stone wall about a metre and a half high," which does not answer.

"Near the turning on the Bat Galim road towards the hospital the Arab Legion is encamped. There were cases in which Arab Legion sentries threatened Jewish nurses and their escorts at gunpoint as they passed, chiefly in the evening — the threat reaching the point of loading a round into the barrel." And: "several armed Arabs stopped a nurse and pointed a pistol at her; only when it became clear to them that this nurse was an Arab did they let her go."

HaMashkif, 26 January 1948 · · the settlement that followed left two gates open with one British and one Arab policeman at each and six more British police inside — no Jewish guard; the Yishuv institutions judged it insufficient

Davar carries the same inquiry with two details HaMashkif omits — the Jewish staff's "pay was meagre" and their conditions poor, and "the hospital was made ownerless; the murderous gangs infused into it a spirit of terror. The health authorities did not heed the warnings, and when the employees felt their lives to be in danger they left."

And a partial return, four weeks later

"Eight Jewish doctors at the Government Hospital in Haifa, and 20 clerks who work in the health department there, returned yesterday to their work. The nurses and Jewish trainee nurses have not yet returned." So: patients and staff out on 31 December 1947, doctors and clerks back about 28 January 1948, nurses not. Any discontinuity in the ledgers across that seam now has a documented cause and a date on either side — and the register's three last Jewish admissions (13 January, 2 February, 23 February 1948) fall on the far side of it.

The handover, May 1948

"When the Government Hospital at Bat Galim was occupied, it was found that all equipment, including ambulances and medical supplies, had been stolen, and that the lifts and electric power system had been damaged. The Vaad Leumi allocated £P.10,000 for renovation and supplies. Already the top floor, which formerly housed the British section, has been put back into operation with 40 beds… One of the first patients treated at the hospital when it was re-opened was an Arab."

PP, 28 May 1948 ·

Three weeks earlier the outcome had still been open. Six days after the fall of Arab Haifa, Dr A. Katznelson of the Va'ad Leumi's health department met "Dr McLean, director of the government health office, and Dr Hamzi, director of the Government Hospital," and announced the institutions' decision "to ensure the orderly continuation of the Government Hospital for the benefit of the population in general and the Arab population in particular," asking the Arab personnel to continue without fear. Dr Hamzi is Dr Naif Hamzeh — twelve years in these pages, from the 1936 trial to the last week.

It did not hold. The fullest account of the terminus is Haaretz's, and it is worth reading whole:

"The Government Hospital in Haifa was transferred yesterday to the authority of the Haifa municipality… This hospital contains 288 beds and is one of the largest and finest hospitals in the country.

"Our correspondent, who visited the hospital yesterday, found it completely empty. Apart from the joint guard of Jews and British policemen, a few individual British doctors and British nurses, there is not a living soul in it… immediately upon the conquest of Haifa the Arab employees and patients began to leave the hospital in masses. Many patients were transferred by ambulance to the environs of Haifa, many others were taken by boat to Acre. About 60 Arabs, the last of the patients, were taken out of the hospital last Thursday. With them went the last of the nurses, most of them Armenian. The Arab doctors also left…

"Despite his personal promise, Dr Hamzi's ambulances did not return. Dr Hamzi has left for Lebanon. By contrast, praise is due to the conduct of the British chief physician, Dr MacNeil, and of the property inspector, Mr Dook (an Arab, a British subject), thanks to whom the equipment stores were preserved intact.

"The interior of the hospital arouses nausea. The great halls, formerly gleaming with cleanliness, are filthy and befouled. Here and there stretchers still lie stained with blood. In the mortuary four Arab bodies were still lying last night. The beds are overturned; in the nurses' room there is no sign of order. In several departments part of the equipment is missing."

Haaretz, 3 May 1948 ·

The next day the Va'ad Leumi declared itself ready "to take upon themselves the care also of the Arab patients and wounded who are in the hospital" and put £10,000 at the municipality's disposal, the transfer made "under the auspices of the International Red Cross" ; on 7 May the mayor, Shabtai Levy, "appealed to all the Arab employees of the Government Hospital… and said that he is responsible for their personal safety" ; and on 17 May the government departments passed to the municipality, the institution listed among them as "the former Government Hospital" .

Note the disagreement, and keep it

The Palestine Post has the equipment "including ambulances and medical supplies" stolen and the lifts and power damaged. Haaretz, on the same handover, credits the British chief physician and the Arab property inspector with keeping "the equipment stores preserved intact," while allowing that "in several departments part of the equipment is missing." Both were written within a month of each other about the same building. The ambulances are the one item both accounts agree had gone.

The old Mountain Road building had stayed in use as an air-raid casualty post through 1939 (§04). The hospital was renamed Rambam in 1952. The registers begin in 1930 with a hospital that could not get its approach road paved, and end on 2 May 1948 with 288 beds, four bodies in the mortuary and a name in the past tense.

§ 10

What the other periodicals add

Beyond the institutional history, the press corpora do two further things: they validate the register from outside, and — in one important respect — the register corrects them.

The press validates the register, exactly

  • 6 July 1938. After the Haifa market bombing, al-Difa' of 7 July printed the hospital-by-hospital tally: — 33 injured in the Government Hospital, 7 critical. The register holds 34 injury admissions that day, of which one died: a 12-year-old Muslim boy with a bullet wound to chest and abdomen, admitted 6 July, died 7 July. 34 − 1 = 33, exactly the paper's count. An independent contemporaneous source confirming the register's completeness to the person.
  • 18 June 1946. al-Difa' of 20 June names seven wounded men and three women admitted, and says one — Sali Rivel, 24 — died. The register: ten injury admissions, one death.
  • 5 May 1947. Eleven Jewish men aged 17–33 with gunshot wounds, nearly all resident Acre — the aftermath of the Acre citadel prison break. One, aged 20, admitted 5 May, died 6 May, "Punc. W. Abdomen (Oper.) Bowel Injury", notebook 31 page 33. al-Difa' of 7 May: — Nasim 'Abbadi, died the morning of 6 May. Same day, same place, same outcome. The press supplies a name the register's redaction removed.
  • Twelve mass-casualty days stand far above the surrounding baseline data/newspapers/casualty_spikes.tsv, eight with press coverage attached; the diagnoses name the mechanism ("Laceration of Brain Due to Bomb explosion", shrapnel wounds). The register independently records the Mandate bombing campaign in Haifa.

And the press dates the gap from outside

The one place the register cannot be asked at all is its four-year gap, and there the press does something the register cannot do for itself. Reporting the demonstrations against the Land Transfer Regulations, the Mitteilungsblatt of 8 March 1940 records a death in our hospital:

"Am Dienstag abend starb im Regierungskrankenhaus in Haifa Lipmann Lipschitz, ein 27-jähriger aus Lettland stammender Jude, an den Verletzungen, die er bei der Demonstration erlitten hatte. An dem Begräbnis am Mittwoch nahmen unabsehbare Menschenmassen teil."

"On Tuesday evening Lipmann Lipschitz, a 27-year-old Jew originally from Latvia, died in the Government Hospital in Haifa of the injuries he suffered at the demonstration. Immense crowds attended the burial on Wednesday."

MB der Hitachdut Olej Germania we Austria, 8 March 1940, p.4 · · Jüdische Weltrundschau carries the same event on 11 March

Tuesday evening is 5 March 1940. Notebook 24 ends on 1 March 1940, and nothing in the general series resumes until Notebook 29 on 8 February 1944, so this death falls four days inside the gap and cannot be checked against the register at all. That is the point of it. The item establishes from outside that on 5 March 1940 the hospital was admitting patients and its deaths were being reported in the press — which means the missing notebooks are missing, not never-written. It is the only external fix on the gap the project has so far.

Honest negatives, kept

30 September 1938 shows nine injuries, several by gunshot, and no press coverage at all. The scalded railwayman reported by Filastin on 5 June 1947 has no matching register row — the only burn that day is a six-year-old boy.

And the register corrects the press

al-Difa' of 21 October 1947 reported four children admitted with diphtheria as the disease returning to Haifa, with a jab at the Department of Health. The register holds a continuous run of diphtheria admissions through September and October 1947, with several child deaths among them. It never went away; the paper noticed only when four arrived together.

Generalised across eleven diseases pipeline/epidemic_concordance.py, the pattern holds. Of 102 strictly Haifa-datelined press disease reports, 73 were printed while the register was already admitting that disease — a median lag behind the register's own surge of 45 days. Only six of sixty register surges were reported in the Haifa press at all. Plague is the one disease the press led, by eleven days in July 1944 — and it was the one that carried a quarantine proclamation. The silences are themselves results: dysentery, influenza and measles are essentially never mentioned. Printability tracked quarantine, not deaths.

Except for the plague, which the Hebrew press counts alongside the register

The 1944–45 Haifa plague outbreak is the one episode where the press does not lag. It followed the outbreak case by case, with running totals and outcomes, and the Government Hospital is the only place of treatment named throughout.

This revises the lead–lag finding above

Plague was already the one disease the press led on, by eleven days in July 1944. The Hebrew series shows why that understates it: for this outbreak the press is not reporting late and thinly, it is counting continuously, by community and by outcome, for eighteen months. The generalisation — press lags the register by a median 45 days, and only six of sixty surges are reported at all — holds for the ordinary-ward diseases. It does not hold for the quarantinable ones, where reporting tracked the quarantine proclamation.

Three caveats that must travel with those numbers

The register is absent from March 1940 to February 1944 and for all of 1945, so most Haifa press windows have no register to compare against. The Arabic corpus is only the pages already matching مستشفى الحكومة + حيفا, so the silences need re-testing against an unrestricted harvest before print. And the lag figures cover only the seven ordinary-ward diseases — the quarantinable three are uninterpreted pending §08.

§ 11

What the hospital was, besides a place of admission

The register records admissions. The Hebrew corpus, read rather than counted, shows an institution doing a great deal that never reaches an admission line — and two of those functions bear directly on how the register itself should be read.

It was the district mortuary and the medico-legal examiner

Bodies arrive throughout, from 1935 to 1948, and they are not admissions: a sailor washed ashore near Kfar Samir, "taken for examination to the Government Hospital" ; an electrocuted porter sent to "the mortuary beside the Government Hospital" ; the Patria dead going "straight to the mortuary of the Government Hospital," 41 buried by 5 December 1940 . Funerals leave from it — after the June 1939 market bomb, fifteen to Balad al-Sheikh, two to Safed, one to Shefa-Amr . And in January 1948 it advertised itself as an identification service by telephone: "Anyone who knows of a missing Jew should apply to the Hevra Kadisha in Haifa (telephone 3923)" .

Unnamed entries in the ledger have documented causes

This is the finding that changes how a blank name column should be read. Four distinct mechanisms, all attested:

  • Mass casualty outstripping identification. After the raid of 20 September 1940, "13 of the victims of the bombardment were not buried at once because they were not identified. When they were identified they were buried in the cemetery at Balad al-Sheikh. Four more victims remained unidentified."
  • A burial formula for the unidentified. Two ma'apilim were buried as "Abraham son of Abraham" — the unknown man of the Lanegev in February 1947, whose pockets held a comb, a cigarette case and an empty notebook , and one of two Knesset Yisrael immigrants on whom the hospital's own physician performed the autopsies . Anyone reading unnamed entries in the 1946–47 ledgers should know the formula existed.
  • Bodies whose community could not be determined. A headless body lay in the mortuary for days in January 1948: "It has not yet been possible to determine whether the body is a Jew's or an Arab's." A boy of thirteen under a lorry could not be identified "because the boy's head was crushed" — the only thing on him a letter from a Hashomer Hatzair branch .
  • Foundlings. Three in this corpus, and they are admissions: a child of three months found beside the hospital in April 1939 ; a day-old girl abandoned in Nazareth Street and brought in, whose mother Sara Hussein was tried and her sentence reduced ; and "a day-old baby found dead beside the gate" in January 1947 .

It had a prisoners' wing, and bodies could leave it the way they came

The chaplain to prisons visited "the cell of the male and female prisoners in the Government Hospital in Haifa," finding that "the wounded receive the ordinary medical care of the Government Hospital" . After the Acre prison break of May 1947, named men were moved back and forth between prison and "the prisoners' wing" . An armed attempt to break into the hospital was foiled in September 1946 . And the reverse: in January 1947 some two hundred relatives of a murdered café keeper, "finding the gate shut, climbed over the fence, entered the hospital, took the body out over the fence" and carried it home .

From 1946 it was the port's examining station for the ma'apilim

The sick were landed, examined and sorted there before Atlit. Of 45 sick immigrants left in the country in September 1946, six went to the Government Hospital, named with their departments — maternity, surgical, infectious diseases . After the Exodus in July 1947, "the sick sent to the Government Hospital are 17 men, 10 women and one child," with 34 relatives as escorts; three died, and "the dead were brought to the hospital half-naked" . Three graves were dug on 21 July and the bodies carried from the mortuary wrapped in blue-and-white flags.

And a maternity ward, a nursing school, two bus routes

A baby a few hours old vanished from the children's room in May 1947 and was found alive a week later, wrapped in "the Government Hospital's clothes" in a house in Wadi Rushmiya; the woman who took her, childless after five years of marriage and threatened by her husband, told the judge she had meant to raise her as her own . The Palestine Broadcasting Service ran "a 'By Request' programme specially for the nurses of the Government Hospital in Haifa" . And the hospital terminated two named bus routes — number 5 (East Haifa — the Government Hospital) in 1943 and number 7, "from Bat Galim via the Government Hospital to the eastern railway station," in 1945 . Between 1943 and 1945 it is a fixed point of the municipal transport map.

A judge found the institution negligent — twice in six weeks

"This is the second case brought before me in the last six weeks in which wounded men lose their lives through negligence on the part of the Government Hospital," said Judge Khalil Shehadeh in the Haifa district court. "The treatment of poor people brought to the Government Hospital is not satisfactory; they discharge the patients before they have properly recovered. Had they been cured properly, it is possible they would have gone on living." The case was Rashwan Awab, wounded in a quarrel, admitted in January, discharged after a fortnight, brought back when his wounds suppurated, and dead there; the judge reduced the assailant's sentence "after the death was caused by the hospital's neglect."

A death in the ledger whose cause a court assigned to the institution itself. Hold this beside any mortality figure drawn from the register — and beside the discharge dates, which is where the judge said the fault lay.

§ 12

Traps for anyone repeating this research

  • Arabic مار لوقا is Hebron, not Haifa. Of 265 concordance windows around لوقا in Filastin and al-Difa' 1925–1948, thirty-five name الخليل outright; the hospital of that name in the Arabic press is St. Luke's, Hebron. Haifa's مار لوقا after 1937 is the school. data/newspapers/stluke_luqa_concordance.tsv
  • English "Luke" is often the Chief Secretary. Sir Harry Charles Luke, Officer Administering the Government, saturates 1928–29 results.
  • Unqualified المستشفى الحكومي can be another town's — Jaffa, Hebron and Jerusalem all had one. Check the dateline; a Hebron case slipped through once.
  • The dateline trap is worse in German. Most Mandate-era Regierungskrankenhaus hits in the Mitteilungsblatt are other towns' — Jaffa, Tel Aviv, Jerusalem. And Bat Galim is a neighbourhood, so it is a weak query on its own.
  • The Fraktur long s (ſ) is indexed as its own character. In Die Warte des Tempels, Hospital finds one page; Hoſpital finds 42. Any German word with a non-final s misses the Fraktur corpus entirely unless the ſ-form is queried too (Krankenhaus escapes it, its s being word-final). Fold ſ→s before any local regex. This is the German counterpart of the Arabic prefix problem, and it silently turns a real corpus into an apparent absence.
  • Compound-splitting. German compounds break across narrow columns and the index does not rejoin them: in Compact Memory Regierungskrankenhaus returns 109 documents while the adjacency phrase "Regierungs Krankenhaus" finds 44 more it cannot see. Query the split forms too; never read a zero as absence.
  • The compound is not how the papers write it. Across the four German-language Palestine titles, Regierungskrankenhaus yields 4 hits and Regierungshospital none, while Krankenhaus alone yields 271 and Krankenhaus+Haifa 69. An earlier survey of this project measured only the compound and concluded the German press was empty. It is not.
  • German Typhus means typhoid fever, not English typhus (that is Fleckfieber). The register's own wording confirms which is meant.
Source caution

The claim that the St. Luke's hospital building adjoined the church comes from amiramorenbikes.com, an unsourced personal cycling blog whose dates ("1889–1928") are wrong at both ends. The building and its dedication inscription are physical evidence and may be trusted; the narrative around them may not.

§ 13

Testing the reports against the register

The 1933 report's phrase — the expansion was "of great utility to all communities in the district" — is a testable claim, and the register is the only source that can test it.

Finding · the expansion is visible in the register

Admissions run at about 87 a month through January–April 1933, rise through May and June, and settle near 200 a month from July — a doubling that holds for years afterwards (196/mo in 1934, 208/mo in 1935). This is an independent corroboration of the St. Luke's takeover from patient-level data, and the strongest confirmation in this document that §05 is right.

Finding · "all communities" survives contact with the data

Every community gained in absolute terms. Muslim admissions roughly doubled, Christian roughly doubled, and Jewish admissions more than tripled — 17 to 58 a month. Because the smallest group grew fastest, the Jewish share rose from 20.0% to 29.2% while the Muslim share fell from 51.3% to 46.2%. The hospital did not merely get bigger; it became proportionally less dominated by its largest constituency.

Where the register contradicts the 1930 report

MR-1930 §15 says the hospital "can seldom take in patients other than officers and employees of the Government … policemen, prisoners, and accident cases." In 1930–1932 the register's commonest recorded occupations are Labourer (72), Child, Agric., Fellah, Porter, Servant, Scavenger; explicitly governmental occupations are about a sixth of the 485 rows where occupation is recorded.

Two readings are possible and the data cannot yet separate them: either the report describes admission policy and pressure rather than actual intake, or it describes the general wards while the register captures a wider population.

Weak evidence — do not press

Occupation is recorded for only 33% of 1930–32 rows, and just 13% of 1935–38. This finding is suggestive, not settled.

Class composition is stable across the break

Third class is 88.8% of admissions in 1930, 89.8% in 1932, 84.1% in 1933 and 84.3% in 1934 — the expansion widened access without changing the hospital's social character. The decisive shift comes much later: third class falls to 72.8% (1944), 69.6% (1946) and 66.1% (1947) as second class rises from about a tenth to a quarter or more.

The most economical reading is that the Government Hospital's Jewish share tracked the availability of Jewish voluntary alternatives rather than any change in government policy:

  • 1930–32, 30–36%. Hadassah Haifa closed for general cases in 1931, reopening only for maternity and children — Jewish patients had fewer alternatives.
  • 1933–35, 25–27%. The St. Luke's expansion; all communities served.
  • 1936, 21.0%. Kupat Cholim's new 50-bed hospital opened in October 1936 .
  • 1937–38, 16.8% and 16.4%. Jewish voluntary provision now substantial.
  • 1939–40, 21.9% and 19.8%. A counter-movement in the year the 220-bed Bat Galim building came into use — suggesting capacity, not only communal provision, had been constraining who got in.
  • 1944–47, 10.3% down to 7.1%, and 1948, 0.4% — three Jewish admissions, the last on 23 February. Not attrition but an administrative removal, reported at the time by the Mitteilungsblatt (§09).
1948 is not an artefact — it is a documented act

An earlier draft of this document cautioned that the near-absence of Jewish patients in 1948 was a boundary condition of the source: four covered months from a single notebook, coinciding with the city's partition. That caution was wrong. The Mitteilungsblatt of 23 January 1948 reports that Jewish patients "had to be removed from the Government Hospital for security reasons," and the register's monthly series matches: 1 admission in January, 2 in February, none thereafter, the last on 23 February (§09). Thin coverage is real, but it is not what produced the 0.4%.

What remains open

  • The lease. MR-1935 says the premises were rented, but the landlord, term and rent are unevidenced. MECA 119/1 (1933–37), 119/2A (Acquisition of Site, 1937–39) and 36/1 (J&EM property in Haifa, 1893–1956) should hold it. Access is Open, with "no restrictions on copying or quotation"; remote users need MECA's paid digitisation service.
  • A box-number check. Mahmoud cites the 1928–29 annual report as "Box 75a/2," but per JEM-cat 75A/2 holds Vols 3–4 (1903–1915); the 1926–1930 reports are Vol 7 in 75B/2.
  • The lazaret referral arrangement (§08) — whether suspected cholera, plague and smallpox cases went to the Kerentina rather than the hospital. Narrowed, not closed. The Israel State Archives files have now been read: they establish that the lazaret and an infectious block were planned as one adjoining project in 1928 and deferred , and that the 1942 plague unit was built on the hospital site itself . What is still missing is the referral paperwork for the 1930s; until it turns up, those three diseases stay uninterpreted before 1942.
  • The decade before the registers. The Haifa Department of Health kept a nominal register of infectious disease from December 1921 to December 1928 — patient, parents, residence, disease, date of recovery or death — roughly 7,200 named cases covering the years the admission registers do not reach . It is only partly extracted, and it is the largest piece of unworked evidence in the project.
  • A lost 1939 notebook, roughly 19 June – 31 August, some 1,190 admissions, between Notebooks 22 and 23.
  • Where exactly on Mountain Road, in modern terms, and whether the building survives.
  • The 21 versus 22 December 1938 discrepancy.
  • Register No. was never extracted — the ledger's own annual serial would convert "per covered month" into true completeness estimates and test whether the 1933 jump is partly an artefact of notebook survival.

  • Bed strength after 1938 is unevidenced. The table in §06 stops at the 220 beds of October 1938. The later annual reports (post-1938 MR volumes, and the Department of Health's own series, NLI PC 43023) are where this is settled.
